What Sales Actually Is

Most people have the wrong picture of sales before they start. Let's replace it with the real one.

If you think sales is about persuading reluctant people to buy things they don't need, you've absorbed 40 years of bad Hollywood movies. That version of sales — pushy, manipulative, transactional — exists, but it's a dying breed. The sales you're learning is something completely different.

The Real Definition

Sales is the discipline of understanding someone's problem well enough to know whether your solution genuinely solves it — and then communicating that clearly.

Notice what's absent from that definition: pressure, tricks, manipulation. The best salespeople are part therapist, part business analyst, part trusted advisor. Their deals close because the fit is real — not because they wore someone down.

Why This Matters For You

Sales is the highest-paid entry-level profession in business. An SDR (Sales Development Rep) with 12 months' experience can earn more than a graduate in most other fields. An Account Executive with 3 years' experience can earn £80,000–£150,000+ including commission. Skills transfer across every industry. And unlike coding or finance, the runway to senior income is extremely fast.

The Sales Funnel Reality

Every B2B company runs some version of this funnel. Understanding it tells you exactly where you'd fit as a new hire.

Awareness → Interest → Consideration → Decision → Revenue

In a typical SaaS company, the sales team is responsible for Interest through Decision. Marketing handles Awareness. Account Management handles Revenue after the deal closes.

The Two Entry Roles

SDR (Sales Development Rep) — also called BDR (Business Development Rep). Your job is to create pipeline. You prospect, you cold call, you cold email, you book meetings. You don't close deals. Success is measured in qualified meetings booked.

AE (Account Executive) — runs the full sales cycle from qualified meeting to signed contract. Manages demos, discovery, proposals, negotiations. You'd typically reach AE after 12–18 months as an SDR.

Sales Career Paths

Sales isn't one job — it's a family of roles. Picking the right starting point makes everything easier.

The most common mistake career-changers make is applying for Account Executive roles before they're ready. AEs need to run complex conversations, manage multiple stakeholders, and navigate long sales cycles. That takes experience. The smart path is through an SDR or BDR role first — you earn while you learn the craft.

The Core Sales Roles

📞
SDR / BDR
Outbound prospecting, cold outreach, booking discovery calls. The entry point for most sales careers.
🎯
Account Executive (AE)
Full sales cycle: discovery → demo → proposal → close. The main revenue-generating role.
🌱
Customer Success (CSM)
Retains and grows existing customers. Great for relationship-focused people who prefer less cold outreach.
🔧
Sales Engineer
Technical expert who supports AEs in complex deals. Ideal if you have a technical background.
📊
Sales Ops / RevOps
Runs the tools, data, and processes that make the team efficient. More analytical and strategic.
👑
Sales Manager / VP
Leads a team of reps. More coaching and strategy, less direct selling.

How SaaS Sales Works

The language of SaaS sales is numbers. Learn the metrics and you'll sound like an insider from day one.

SaaS stands for Software as a Service — subscription software delivered over the internet. Unlike traditional software (you buy a licence once), SaaS charges monthly or annually. This fundamentally changes how sales teams are measured.

The Key Metrics

ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) — the total value of all annual subscriptions. If a company has 100 customers paying £12,000/year each, ARR = £1.2m. This is the number that matters most to investors and leadership.

MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue) — ARR divided by 12. Used to track growth month-to-month. MRR going up = good. MRR going down = churn problem.

ACV (Average Contract Value) — the average annual value of a deal. Low ACV (£1k–5k) means high-volume transactional sales. High ACV (£50k–500k+) means enterprise sales with long cycles.

Churn Rate — the percentage of customers who cancel each year. 5% annual churn means you lose 1 in 20 customers. High churn destroys ARR growth even if you're winning new deals constantly.

Pipeline — the total value of all deals currently in your sales process. A healthy pipeline is typically 3–4× your quota. If your quota is £100k, you need £300–400k in pipeline to hit it.

Quota — your sales target. Usually expressed as ARR closed per quarter or year. SDR quotas are typically measured in meetings booked or pipeline generated.

Building A Sales CV

Your CV isn't a job history. It's a sales document — and you're the product.

The biggest mistake career-changers make on their sales CV is writing responsibilities instead of results. Nobody hiring a sales rep cares that you "managed customer relationships." They care that you grew them by 40%.

The Sales CV Formula

Every bullet point on your CV should follow this structure: Action verb → What you did → Measurable result → Context/scale

Examples of weak vs strong bullets:

❌ Weak

"Responsible for managing client accounts and handling customer queries across the retail team."

✅ Strong

"Managed 34 retail accounts worth £1.2m annual revenue, resolving 95%+ of queries within SLA and achieving 91% renewal rate across the portfolio."

Transferable Skills That Map to Sales

Most people have more relevant experience than they realise. Here's how to translate it:

Retail / hospitalityRapport, objection handling"Upsold to 30% of customers through needs-based recommendations"
RecruitmentPipeline management, cold outreach"Managed pipeline of 80+ candidates, made 40 cold calls daily"
MarketingICP understanding, messaging"Created campaigns targeting 3 buyer personas, generating 200+ MQLs/month"
Teaching / trainingExplanation, consultative approach"Adapted communication style to 30+ students with different learning needs"

LinkedIn Optimisation

Sales hiring managers live on LinkedIn. Your profile is your first impression — make it count.

90% of B2B sales recruiters use LinkedIn as their primary sourcing tool. If your profile isn't optimised, you're invisible. Worse, a generic profile actively signals you don't understand sales — because good salespeople know how to position themselves.

The 6 Elements That Matter

1. Headline — Don't write your job title. Write your value proposition. Most people write "Marketing Manager at ACME Corp." Instead, write something like: "Aspiring SDR | 2 years B2B customer-facing | Studying consultative selling | Open to opportunities."

2. Profile Photo — Professional, clear, smiling. A good photo increases profile views by 21×. Dress as you would for an interview.

3. About Section — Tell your story in 3–4 short paragraphs. Why sales? What transferable experience do you have? What are you actively doing to prepare? What kind of role are you looking for?

4. Experience — Use the CV formula from Module 4. Every role should have result-oriented bullets.

5. Skills — Add: Cold Calling, Prospecting, CRM (Salesforce/HubSpot), Business Development, Account Management. These are keyword-searched by recruiters.

6. Activity — Post once a week about what you're learning. This signals genuine interest and gets you noticed. A post like "I just finished Module 5 of SalesLab. Here's what surprised me about the SDR interview process..." will land you in recruiter feeds.

💡 The 500+ Connections Threshold

LinkedIn treats profiles with 500+ connections differently — you show up in more searches and appear more credible. Spend one hour connecting with SDRs, AEs, and recruiters in your target sector. Send a 1-line note: "Building my network in SaaS sales — happy to connect."

All true — but the most powerful effect is demonstrating genuine interest. A recruiter who sees your learning-focused posts will remember you and view you as a motivated, self-driven candidate.

Finding SDR Jobs

Most people apply the slow way. Here's the fast way.

The traditional job search — browse boards, apply, wait — works eventually. But for sales roles specifically, it's the least efficient method. Sales managers respect proactivity. Showing it in your job search is the first test of whether you have sales instincts.

Tier 1: Direct Outreach (Fastest)

Find the VP of Sales or Sales Manager at your target companies directly on LinkedIn. Send a 3-line message:

✅ Template: Cold LinkedIn Outreach to Sales Manager

Hi [Name], I've been following [Company]'s growth — congrats on [recent milestone/funding/expansion]. I'm actively making the move into SDR roles and [Company] is at the top of my list because [specific reason]. Would you be open to a 15-minute call to learn more about your team? Happy to send my CV first.

Tier 2: Job Boards (Broad Coverage)

The best boards for SDR roles: LinkedIn Jobs (set alert for "SDR" + "BDR" + "Sales Development"), Workable, Greenhouse (many scale-ups use this ATS), Glassdoor, and sector-specific boards like SaaStr for SaaS roles.

Tier 3: Recruiters (Passive But Useful)

Specialist sales recruiters: Aspire (UK-focused), SalesRecruit, Pareto Law (SDR-specific). Connect and book a call — they'll send you relevant roles you won't find on boards.

The 5-Company Target System

Don't spray and pray. Pick 5 target companies. Research each one. Write personalised outreach. Track your pipeline in a spreadsheet: Company | Contact | Outreach Date | Response | Status. This is literally how SDRs work — running their own job search as a sales process is excellent preparation and makes a great interview story.

The SDR Interview

SDR interviews test three things: coachability, resilience, and preparation. Here's how to demonstrate all three.

Most SDR interviews follow a predictable structure. If you know what's coming, you can prepare specific stories and practice until they feel natural rather than rehearsed.

Stage 1: Recruiter Screen (20–30 min)

The recruiter is filtering for culture fit and basic competence. They want to know you're enthusiastic, you understand what the role involves, and you can communicate clearly. Common questions: "Why sales?", "Why this company?", "Walk me through your background."

Stage 2: Hiring Manager Interview (45–60 min)

This is where you need substance. The Sales Manager is assessing whether they'd trust you with prospects. They'll use structured questions (STAR method) to get evidence, not stories. Common questions: "Tell me about a time you faced rejection and how you handled it," "What does a great discovery call look like to you?", "How do you prepare for a cold call?"

Stage 3: Mock Cold Call / Role Play

Many companies give you a mock scenario: "Sell me this pen" or a realistic product scenario. They're not testing whether you close the sale — they're testing whether you ask questions before pitching. The trap is pitching immediately. The correct response is discovery first.

Stage 4: Final / Panel (sometimes)

For competitive roles you may meet the team or present a 30/60/90-day plan. Prepare a simple deck: What you'll learn in Month 1, what you'll produce in Month 2, what you'll own in Month 3.

Prospect For Your Own Job

The best way to demonstrate you can sell is to use the exact same process to get the job. Here's how.

Here's an insight most career-changers miss: the process for landing a sales job is identical to the process for booking a sales meeting. You have a product (you), a set of target accounts (companies you want to work for), and a buyer (the hiring manager). Run it like a sales campaign.

Step 1: Build Your ICP

Your ICP (Ideal Company Profile) is the type of company most likely to hire you given your background and what you want. Think about: company size (50–300 people scales best for SDRs), funding stage (Series A–C = hiring aggressively), sector (pick one you understand), location or remote policy, and whether they have a proper SDR function vs expecting you to learn alone.

Step 2: Build Your Target List

Use LinkedIn, Crunchbase, and G2 to find 20–30 companies that fit your ICP. Record them in a spreadsheet: Company | Size | Stage | SDR Roles Open | Hiring Manager | Contact Found | Outreach Sent | Response.

Step 3: Sequence Your Outreach

Don't send one LinkedIn message and wait. Run a sequence:

Day 1: Connect with the Sales Manager on LinkedIn (no message yet). Day 3: Send the personalised cold message (Module 6 template). Day 7: Follow-up if no response: "Just bumping this up — really interested in [specific thing]. Happy to be brief." Day 14: One final follow-up referencing something new: a company announcement, their recent LinkedIn post, etc.

Step 4: Track Everything

Your pipeline management is your interview story. When they ask "how have you been approaching your job search?" you say: "I built a 25-company target list, ran a 3-touch outreach sequence, tracked it all in a pipeline spreadsheet, and that's how I'm here today." That answer gets you hired.

The Consultative Mindset

The shift from product-pusher to trusted advisor starts with a single change in how you enter every conversation.

Transactional selling asks: "How do I get this person to buy?" Consultative selling asks: "What is this person actually trying to achieve — and is my solution the right fit?"

These two questions lead to entirely different behaviours. The transactional rep talks more, pitches earlier, and treats objections as obstacles. The consultative rep asks more questions, listens more than they talk, and treats objections as information.

The Four Principles

1. Curiosity over conviction. Go into every call with genuine questions, not predetermined answers. Your job is to understand before you recommend.

2. Problems over products. Buyers don't care about your product. They care about their problem. Every conversation should start there.

3. Long-term over short-term. Pushing a deal that isn't a fit might win the quarter but destroys trust and creates churn. A genuine fit compounds.

4. Evidence over assertion. Don't tell buyers your product is great. Show them proof. Case studies, data, live demos, customer references.

The 70/30 Rule

In a consultative discovery call, your buyer should talk 70% of the time. You talk 30%. If you're talking more than 30%, you're pitching too early.

Discovery & SPIN Selling

SPIN Selling — developed by Neil Rackham after studying 35,000 sales calls — is the most evidence-based sales framework ever created.

In the 1970s, Xerox commissioned a £1m research project to answer one question: what do top salespeople do differently? The answer, after 12 years of research across 35,000 sales calls, was SPIN Selling. The framework describes four types of questions that lead a buyer from mild interest to committed action.

The Four SPIN Question Types

S — Situation Questions establish context. They help you understand where the buyer is today: their team structure, current tools, processes, and scale. Use sparingly — too many feel like an interrogation.

P — Problem Questions surface difficulties, dissatisfactions, and frustrations. They shift the buyer from neutral to aware of pain. "How much time does your team currently spend on manual reporting?" "What breaks down most often in your current process?"

I — Implication Questions amplify the pain. They connect the problem to downstream consequences — the real cost of not solving it. "If that process breaks down on a quarter-end, what's the knock-on effect for leadership reporting?" This is where emotional urgency is created.

N — Need-Payoff Questions invite the buyer to articulate the value of the solution themselves. "If you could automate that entirely, what would that mean for the team?" When the buyer says the value out loud, they own it. It's vastly more powerful than you saying it for them.

⚠️ The Most Common SPIN Mistake

Jumping straight from Situation to Need-Payoff without spending time on Implication. Buyers don't commit to change until the pain of staying the same outweighs the friction of switching. Implication questions do that work.

Active Listening

The most underrated skill in sales. Everyone claims to do it. Almost nobody actually does.

Active listening isn't waiting for the buyer to stop talking so you can respond. It's the deliberate act of understanding not just what someone says — but what they mean, what they feel, and what they're not saying.

The Four Levels of Listening

Level 1 — Passive: You hear the words. You're already planning your response. You miss nuance, emotion, and subtext.

Level 2 — Selective: You listen for the things you expected to hear. You confirm your hypotheses. You miss surprises.

Level 3 — Active: You track meaning, emotion, and pattern. You notice what's emphasised, what's repeated, what's avoided. You ask follow-up questions that prove you were listening.

Level 4 — Empathic: You understand the buyer's perspective as if you were in their position. You can voice their concerns better than they can. This is where trust is built.

Techniques That Signal Active Listening

The Echo: Repeat the last 2–3 words of what they said as a question. "—it takes about three days." / "Three days?" They'll almost always elaborate, often revealing something more important.

The Label: Name what you're observing. "It sounds like that's created a lot of extra work for your team." This signals you understand the emotional dimension.

The Pause: After they finish speaking, wait 3 seconds before responding. Most people fill silences — often with their most honest thoughts.

The Summary: Periodically paraphrase: "Let me make sure I've got this right — the main issue is X, which causes Y, and the consequence is Z. Is that right?" This builds trust and confirms understanding.

Building Rapport

Rapport isn't small talk. It's the deliberate building of trust — and in B2B sales, trust is the deal.

People buy from people they trust. In an age of identical SaaS products and comparison tables, the relationship is often the differentiator. Rapport is the foundation of that relationship — but building it effectively requires more than asking "How's your weekend?"

The Three Dimensions of Trust

Credibility — Do they believe you know what you're talking about? Established through preparation, insight, and knowledge of their industry.

Reliability — Do they believe you'll do what you say? Established through following through on small things: sending that article, introducing that contact, responding quickly.

Intimacy — Do they feel you understand their specific situation? Established through listening, remembering details, asking about the person not just the project.

Practical Rapport Techniques

The LinkedIn brief: Before any call, spend 5 minutes on their LinkedIn. Find their tenure, career path, shared connections, recent posts or activity. Reference something specific: "I saw you posted about the challenges of remote sales teams — is that something your team is dealing with currently?"

The Callback: Remember and reference things from previous conversations. "Last time we spoke you mentioned your board meeting was coming up — how did that go?" This signals you see them as a person, not a deal.

The genuine compliment: Not flattery — genuine acknowledgement. "The way you've structured your RevOps team is pretty unusual for a company your size — what drove that decision?" Most people love talking about decisions they're proud of.
