What Sales Actually Is

Most people have the wrong picture of sales before they start. Let's replace it with the real one.

If you think sales is about persuading reluctant people to buy things they don't need, you've absorbed 40 years of bad Hollywood movies. That version of sales — pushy, manipulative, transactional — exists, but it's a dying breed. The sales you're learning is something completely different.

The Real Definition

Sales is the discipline of understanding someone's problem well enough to know whether your solution genuinely solves it — and then communicating that clearly.

Notice what's absent from that definition: pressure, tricks, manipulation. The best salespeople are part therapist, part business analyst, part trusted advisor. Their deals close because the fit is real — not because they wore someone down.

Why This Matters For You

Sales is the highest-paid entry-level profession in business. An SDR (Sales Development Rep) with 12 months' experience can earn more than a graduate in most other fields. An Account Executive with 3 years' experience can earn £80,000–£150,000+ including commission. Skills transfer across every industry. And unlike coding or finance, the runway to senior income is extremely fast.

The Sales Funnel Reality

Every B2B company runs some version of this funnel. Understanding it tells you exactly where you'd fit as a new hire.

Awareness → Interest → Consideration → Decision → Revenue

In a typical SaaS company, the sales team is responsible for Interest through Decision. Marketing handles Awareness. Account Management handles Revenue after the deal closes.

The Two Entry Roles

SDR (Sales Development Rep) — also called BDR (Business Development Rep). Your job is to create pipeline. You prospect, you cold call, you cold email, you book meetings. You don't close deals. Success is measured in qualified meetings booked.

AE (Account Executive) — runs the full sales cycle from qualified meeting to signed contract. Manages demos, discovery, proposals, negotiations. You'd typically reach AE after 12–18 months as an SDR.

Sales Career Paths

Sales isn't one job — it's a family of roles. Picking the right starting point makes everything easier.

The most common mistake career-changers make is applying for Account Executive roles before they're ready. AEs need to run complex conversations, manage multiple stakeholders, and navigate long sales cycles. That takes experience. The smart path is through an SDR or BDR role first — you earn while you learn the craft.

The Core Sales Roles

📞
SDR / BDR
Outbound prospecting, cold outreach, booking discovery calls. The entry point for most sales careers.
🎯
Account Executive (AE)
Full sales cycle: discovery → demo → proposal → close. The main revenue-generating role.
🌱
Customer Success (CSM)
Retains and grows existing customers. Great for relationship-focused people who prefer less cold outreach.
🔧
Sales Engineer
Technical expert who supports AEs in complex deals. Ideal if you have a technical background.
📊
Sales Ops / RevOps
Runs the tools, data, and processes that make the team efficient. More analytical and strategic.
👑
Sales Manager / VP
Leads a team of reps. More coaching and strategy, less direct selling.

How SaaS Sales Works

The language of SaaS sales is numbers. Learn the metrics and you'll sound like an insider from day one.

SaaS stands for Software as a Service — subscription software delivered over the internet. Unlike traditional software (you buy a licence once), SaaS charges monthly or annually. This fundamentally changes how sales teams are measured.

The Key Metrics

ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) — the total value of all annual subscriptions. If a company has 100 customers paying £12,000/year each, ARR = £1.2m. This is the number that matters most to investors and leadership.

MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue) — ARR divided by 12. Used to track growth month-to-month. MRR going up = good. MRR going down = churn problem.

ACV (Average Contract Value) — the average annual value of a deal. Low ACV (£1k–5k) means high-volume transactional sales. High ACV (£50k–500k+) means enterprise sales with long cycles.

Churn Rate — the percentage of customers who cancel each year. 5% annual churn means you lose 1 in 20 customers. High churn destroys ARR growth even if you're winning new deals constantly.

Pipeline — the total value of all deals currently in your sales process. A healthy pipeline is typically 3–4× your quota. If your quota is £100k, you need £300–400k in pipeline to hit it.

Quota — your sales target. Usually expressed as ARR closed per quarter or year. SDR quotas are typically measured in meetings booked or pipeline generated.

Building A Sales CV

Your CV isn't a job history. It's a sales document — and you're the product.

The biggest mistake career-changers make on their sales CV is writing responsibilities instead of results. Nobody hiring a sales rep cares that you "managed customer relationships." They care that you grew them by 40%.

The Sales CV Formula

Every bullet point on your CV should follow this structure: Action verb → What you did → Measurable result → Context/scale

Examples of weak vs strong bullets:

❌ Weak

"Responsible for managing client accounts and handling customer queries across the retail team."

✅ Strong

"Managed 34 retail accounts worth £1.2m annual revenue, resolving 95%+ of queries within SLA and achieving 91% renewal rate across the portfolio."

LinkedIn Optimisation

Sales hiring managers live on LinkedIn. Your profile is your first impression — make it count.

90% of B2B sales recruiters use LinkedIn as their primary sourcing tool. If your profile isn't optimised, you're invisible. Worse, a generic profile actively signals you don't understand sales — because good salespeople know how to position themselves.

The 6 Elements That Matter

1. Headline — Don't write your job title. Write your value proposition. Most people write "Marketing Manager at ACME Corp." Instead, write something like: "Aspiring SDR | 2 years B2B customer-facing | Studying consultative selling | Open to opportunities."

2. Profile Photo — Professional, clear, smiling. A good photo increases profile views by 21×. Dress as you would for an interview.

3. About Section — Tell your story in 3–4 short paragraphs. Why sales? What transferable experience do you have? What are you actively doing to prepare? What kind of role are you looking for?

4. Experience — Use the CV formula from Module 4. Every role should have result-oriented bullets.

5. Skills — Add: Cold Calling, Prospecting, CRM (Salesforce/HubSpot), Business Development, Account Management. These are keyword-searched by recruiters.

6. Activity — Post once a week about what you're learning. This signals genuine interest and gets you noticed. A post like "I just finished Module 5 of SalesLab. Here's what surprised me about the SDR interview process..." will land you in recruiter feeds.

💡 The 500+ Connections Threshold

LinkedIn treats profiles with 500+ connections differently — you show up in more searches and appear more credible. Spend one hour connecting with SDRs, AEs, and recruiters in your target sector. Send a 1-line note: "Building my network in SaaS sales — happy to connect."

Finding SDR Jobs

Most people apply the slow way. Here's the fast way.

The traditional job search — browse boards, apply, wait — works eventually. But for sales roles specifically, it's the least efficient method. Sales managers respect proactivity. Showing it in your job search is the first test of whether you have sales instincts.

Tier 1: Direct Outreach (Fastest)

Find the VP of Sales or Sales Manager at your target companies directly on LinkedIn. Send a 3-line message:

✅ Template: Cold LinkedIn Outreach to Sales Manager

Hi [Name], I've been following [Company]'s growth — congrats on [recent milestone/funding/expansion]. I'm actively making the move into SDR roles and [Company] is at the top of my list because [specific reason]. Would you be open to a 15-minute call to learn more about your team? Happy to send my CV first.

Tier 2: Job Boards (Broad Coverage)

The best boards for SDR roles: LinkedIn Jobs (set alert for "SDR" + "BDR" + "Sales Development"), Workable, Greenhouse (many scale-ups use this ATS), Glassdoor, and sector-specific boards like SaaStr for SaaS roles.

Tier 3: Recruiters (Passive But Useful)

Specialist sales recruiters: Aspire (UK-focused), SalesRecruit, Pareto Law (SDR-specific). Connect and book a call — they'll send you relevant roles you won't find on boards.

The 5-Company Target System

Don't spray and pray. Pick 5 target companies. Research each one. Write personalised outreach. Track your pipeline in a spreadsheet: Company | Contact | Outreach Date | Response | Status. This is literally how SDRs work — running their own job search as a sales process is excellent preparation and makes a great interview story.

The SDR Interview

SDR interviews test three things: coachability, resilience, and preparation. Here's how to demonstrate all three.

Most SDR interviews follow a predictable structure. If you know what's coming, you can prepare specific stories and practice until they feel natural rather than rehearsed.

Stage 1: Recruiter Screen (20–30 min)

The recruiter is filtering for culture fit and basic competence. They want to know you're enthusiastic, you understand what the role involves, and you can communicate clearly. Common questions: "Why sales?", "Why this company?", "Walk me through your background."

Stage 2: Hiring Manager Interview (45–60 min)

This is where you need substance. The Sales Manager is assessing whether they'd trust you with prospects. They'll use structured questions (STAR method) to get evidence, not stories. Common questions: "Tell me about a time you faced rejection and how you handled it," "What does a great discovery call look like to you?", "How do you prepare for a cold call?"

Stage 3: Mock Cold Call / Role Play

Many companies give you a mock scenario: "Sell me this pen" or a realistic product scenario. They're not testing whether you close the sale — they're testing whether you ask questions before pitching. The trap is pitching immediately. The correct response is discovery first.

Stage 4: Final / Panel (sometimes)

For competitive roles you may meet the team or present a 30/60/90-day plan. Prepare a simple deck: What you'll learn in Month 1, what you'll produce in Month 2, what you'll own in Month 3.

Prospect For Your Own Job

The best way to demonstrate you can sell is to use the exact same process to get the job. Here's how.

Here's an insight most career-changers miss: the process for landing a sales job is identical to the process for booking a sales meeting. You have a product (you), a set of target accounts (companies you want to work for), and a buyer (the hiring manager). Run it like a sales campaign.

Step 1: Build Your ICP

Your ICP (Ideal Company Profile) is the type of company most likely to hire you given your background and what you want. Think about: company size (50–300 people scales best for SDRs), funding stage (Series A–C = hiring aggressively), sector (pick one you understand), location or remote policy, and whether they have a proper SDR function vs expecting you to learn alone.

Step 2: Build Your Target List

Use LinkedIn, Crunchbase, and G2 to find 20–30 companies that fit your ICP. Record them in a spreadsheet: Company | Size | Stage | SDR Roles Open | Hiring Manager | Contact Found | Outreach Sent | Response.

Step 3: Sequence Your Outreach

Don't send one LinkedIn message and wait. Run a sequence:

Day 1: Connect with the Sales Manager on LinkedIn (no message yet). Day 3: Send the personalised cold message (Module 6 template). Day 7: Follow-up if no response: "Just bumping this up — really interested in [specific thing]. Happy to be brief." Day 14: One final follow-up referencing something new: a company announcement, their recent LinkedIn post, etc.

Step 4: Track Everything

Your pipeline management is your interview story. When they ask "how have you been approaching your job search?" you say: "I built a 25-company target list, ran a 3-touch outreach sequence, tracked it all in a pipeline spreadsheet, and that's how I'm here today." That answer gets you hired.

The Consultative Mindset

The shift from product-pusher to trusted advisor starts with a single change in how you enter every conversation.

Transactional selling asks: "How do I get this person to buy?" Consultative selling asks: "What is this person actually trying to achieve — and is my solution the right fit?"

These two questions lead to entirely different behaviours. The transactional rep talks more, pitches earlier, and treats objections as obstacles. The consultative rep asks more questions, listens more than they talk, and treats objections as information.

The Four Principles

1. Curiosity over conviction. Go into every call with genuine questions, not predetermined answers. Your job is to understand before you recommend.

2. Problems over products. Buyers don't care about your product. They care about their problem. Every conversation should start there.

3. Long-term over short-term. Pushing a deal that isn't a fit might win the quarter but destroys trust and creates churn. A genuine fit compounds.

4. Evidence over assertion. Don't tell buyers your product is great. Show them proof. Case studies, data, live demos, customer references.

The 70/30 Rule

In a consultative discovery call, your buyer should talk 70% of the time. You talk 30%. If you're talking more than 30%, you're pitching too early.

Discovery & SPIN Selling

SPIN Selling — developed by Neil Rackham after studying 35,000 sales calls — is the most evidence-based sales framework ever created.

In the 1970s, Xerox commissioned a £1m research project to answer one question: what do top salespeople do differently? The answer, after 12 years of research across 35,000 sales calls, was SPIN Selling. The framework describes four types of questions that lead a buyer from mild interest to committed action.

The Four SPIN Question Types

S — Situation Questions establish context. They help you understand where the buyer is today: their team structure, current tools, processes, and scale. Use sparingly — too many feel like an interrogation.

P — Problem Questions surface difficulties, dissatisfactions, and frustrations. They shift the buyer from neutral to aware of pain. "How much time does your team currently spend on manual reporting?" "What breaks down most often in your current process?"

I — Implication Questions amplify the pain. They connect the problem to downstream consequences — the real cost of not solving it. "If that process breaks down on a quarter-end, what's the knock-on effect for leadership reporting?" This is where emotional urgency is created.

N — Need-Payoff Questions invite the buyer to articulate the value of the solution themselves. "If you could automate that entirely, what would that mean for the team?" When the buyer says the value out loud, they own it. It's vastly more powerful than you saying it for them.

⚠️ The Most Common SPIN Mistake

Jumping straight from Situation to Need-Payoff without spending time on Implication. Buyers don't commit to change until the pain of staying the same outweighs the friction of switching. Implication questions do that work.

Active Listening

The most underrated skill in sales. Everyone claims to do it. Almost nobody actually does.

Active listening isn't waiting for the buyer to stop talking so you can respond. It's the deliberate act of understanding not just what someone says — but what they mean, what they feel, and what they're not saying.

The Four Levels of Listening

Level 1 — Passive: You hear the words. You're already planning your response. You miss nuance, emotion, and subtext.

Level 2 — Selective: You listen for the things you expected to hear. You confirm your hypotheses. You miss surprises.

Level 3 — Active: You track meaning, emotion, and pattern. You notice what's emphasised, what's repeated, what's avoided. You ask follow-up questions that prove you were listening.

Level 4 — Empathic: You understand the buyer's perspective as if you were in their position. You can voice their concerns better than they can. This is where trust is built.

Techniques That Signal Active Listening

The Echo: Repeat the last 2–3 words of what they said as a question. "—it takes about three days." / "Three days?" They'll almost always elaborate, often revealing something more important.

The Label: Name what you're observing. "It sounds like that's created a lot of extra work for your team." This signals you understand the emotional dimension.

The Pause: After they finish speaking, wait 3 seconds before responding. Most people fill silences — often with their most honest thoughts.

The Summary: Periodically paraphrase: "Let me make sure I've got this right — the main issue is X, which causes Y, and the consequence is Z. Is that right?" This builds trust and confirms understanding.

Building Rapport

Rapport isn't small talk. It's the deliberate building of trust — and in B2B sales, trust is the deal.

People buy from people they trust. In an age of identical SaaS products and comparison tables, the relationship is often the differentiator. Rapport is the foundation of that relationship — but building it effectively requires more than asking "How's your weekend?"

Credibility — Do they believe you know what you're talking about? Established through preparation, insight, and knowledge of their industry.

Reliability — Do they believe you'll do what you say? Established through following through on small things: sending that article, introducing that contact, responding quickly.

Intimacy — Do they feel you understand their specific situation? Established through listening, remembering details, asking about the person not just the project.

The genuine compliment: Not flattery — genuine acknowledgement. "The way you've structured your RevOps team is pretty unusual for a company your size — what drove that decision?" Most people love talking about decisions they're proud of.

Understanding the SDR Role

The SDR role is the most important two years of your entire sales career — not because it pays the most, but because the habits you build here shape how you sell for the next decade.

The SDR function exists because the AE's time is the most expensive variable in a sales organisation. An AE managing a six-month enterprise sales cycle earning £80,000 base has a fully-loaded cost of roughly £120,000+ per year. If that AE spends 40% of their time prospecting, the company is paying £48,000+ per year for prospecting output that an SDR could deliver at £28,000.

Understanding this changes how you approach the role: The SDR is not a junior salesperson. They are a specialist with a distinct function. The SDR's product is a qualified meeting. The SDR's KPIs are volume and conversion — not revenue.

What "Qualified" Actually Means

A qualified meeting is not a meeting with someone who agreed to a call. It is a meeting with a person in the right role at the right company, who has a problem your product solves, who has the authority or access to authority to eventually make a decision, and who agreed to the meeting knowing what it is about.

SDRs who book meetings that do not meet this standard waste AE time and damage the SDR-AE relationship. Meeting quality is as important as meeting volume.

The tragedy of most SDR roles: SDRs are measured exclusively on quantity — dials, emails, meetings booked — without being coached on quality. The result: they develop high-volume habits without developing judgment, and they arrive at AE unable to qualify well. The SDRs who accelerate fastest treat every call as a deliberate practice session, not a dial quota.

Cold Calling Mastery

Cold calling is not dying. It is being abandoned by SDRs who fear rejection. For the rep willing to develop genuine skill, the lack of competition in the channel is an opportunity.

Email open rates in B2B: 20–35%. Cold call connect rates with senior buyers: 8–15%. On the surface, email looks more efficient. But a cold call that reaches the right person can produce a meeting in four minutes. The SDR who has a 10-minute conversation learns more about fit in those 10 minutes than in six email exchanges.

A script vs. a framework: A script is a fixed sequence to recite — it sounds rehearsed because it is. A framework is a set of principles guiding a natural conversation. Cold calling mastery means building a framework, not memorising a script.

The Seven-Element Cold Call Framework

1. Pattern Interrupt (0–5 sec) — Name the cold call before they can. "Hi Sarah, this is Alex at NorthPeak — I will be upfront, this is a cold call. I have a specific reason for reaching out. Do you have 90 seconds?" This creates an unexpected honesty that earns a hearing.

2. Permission Ask — "Have I caught you at a bad moment?" gives them control. If yes: "When would be better to call back?" Do not hang up.

3. Research Hook (10–25 sec) — One specific observation signalling you did three minutes of research: "I saw Cascade Retail just announced a third distribution centre — I wanted to ask how that is affecting your inventory analytics setup." The hook gives them a thread to pull.

4. Problem Frame (25–45 sec) — A tight hypothesis about the specific problem your ICP faces — without pitching the solution yet: "Most Operations Directors at companies in your scale range tell us they are dealing with manual reporting consolidation across sites. I was not sure if that was showing up for you."

5. The Curious Pause (45–60 sec) — Stop. Wait. The prospect is deciding how to respond. Filling that space is the most common cold call mistake. The pause is where the conversation begins.

6. Listen and Qualify (1–4 min) — If they respond with interest, your job is to listen and develop — not to pitch. "Can you say more about that?" "What makes that a priority right now versus three months ago?"

7. Close to a Specific Meeting — "Based on what you have described, I think it is worth a 25-minute conversation. What does your diary look like on Thursday?"

🎯 Expert Insight

The goal of the cold call is not to sell the product. It is to sell the meeting. If your goal is to sell the product, you will pitch. If your goal is to sell the meeting, you will listen. The entire pitch should be: here is the problem I think you might have — does that resonate? Then let us explore together. That is it.

SPIN Selling — The Four Question Types

Neil Rackham studied 35,000 sales calls over 12 years. He found one pattern above all others: the reps who consistently closed large deals asked a fundamentally different type of question than reps who closed small deals.

SPIN is not a script. It is a diagnostic framework built around four types of question, used in sequence to move a prospect from passive awareness of a problem to active desire to solve it. The acronym stands for Situation, Problem, Implication, and Need-Payoff.

The Four Question Types

Situation Questions build baseline context — about the prospect's current environment, processes, and tools. They are necessary but expensive: each one spends a unit of your prospect's patience. Over-reliance on situation questions signals to the buyer that you did not do your research.

Problem Questions explore difficulties, dissatisfactions, and frustrations with the current state. They move from describing the situation to identifying pain. "Where does the current process break down?" "What takes longer than it should?" "What generates the most errors?"

Implication Questions are the most powerful and least used. They explore the downstream consequences of the problem: "What happens to reporting timelines when the manual consolidation is late?" "How does that affect the leadership team's planning?" The implication question transforms a mild inconvenience into a business problem with a cost — making the problem feel more urgent and serious. Senior buyers respond most strongly to implications because they think in terms of business outcomes, not process friction.

Need-Payoff Questions invite the prospect to articulate the value of a solution — in their own words. "If you could eliminate that manual step, what would that enable your team to do instead?" The power is that the prospect is now selling themselves. When a buyer describes the benefit in their own language, their commitment to solving the problem increases dramatically.

BANT — The Qualification Framework

BANT was developed by IBM in the 1950s. It has outlasted nearly every sales methodology invented since. Not because it is perfect — it is not — but because its four dimensions remain the four things that must be true for a deal to close.

BANT stands for Budget, Authority, Need, and Timeline. Each dimension is a gate. A deal missing one can still progress — but the gap must be understood and a strategy built around it, not ignored.

B — Budget

Budget qualification does not mean asking "do you have £50,000?" It means understanding whether spend is possible, who controls it, and whether the problem is sufficiently painful to justify creating budget if none exists. In many mid-market deals, budget is not pre-allocated — it is created when the need is urgent enough. The qualification question is: is this organisation capable of making this level of investment, and is the pain severe enough that they would?

A — Authority

Authority is rarely one person. Modern B2B purchase decisions involve an average of 6.8 stakeholders (Gartner). Authority qualification means mapping the decision-making unit: who signs, who blocks, who influences, who implements. The rep who sells only to their champion — and does not know the full DMU — loses deals they did not see coming.

N — Need

Need qualification goes beyond confirming that a pain exists. It asks: is this pain being actively felt, or is it acknowledged but tolerated? A pain that is tolerated does not generate urgency. A pain that is actively felt creates a deal. The SPIN framework sits inside the Need dimension of BANT — it is how you develop need from awareness into urgency.

T — Timeline

Timeline is the most commonly misqualified dimension. Reps often ask "what is your timeline?" and accept "Q3" as a real answer. A genuine timeline is attached to a business event: a contract renewal, a board presentation, a regulatory deadline, a new hire starting. Timelines without business events are wishes, not commitments.

🎯 Expert Insight

The most common BANT failure: treating it as a checkbox. 'Do they have budget? Yes. Do they have authority? Yes. Do they have need? Yes. Timeline? Q3. Qualified.' Then the deal stalls. BANT is not a checklist — it is a diagnostic framework. For each dimension, the question is not 'does it exist?' but 'how strong is it, what is the evidence, and what is the risk if I am wrong?'

The BANT Diagnostic Conversation

BANT only works if the questions feel like genuine curiosity, not an interrogation. Two AEs running the same BANT framework get completely different results based on how they ask.

🏢 Case Study — Two AEs — Same Prospect — Different Outcomes

James Patel, Head of Operations at a 300-person logistics company (Apex Logistics), takes two discovery calls in the same week. Both AEs are running BANT. SDR1 opens: 'Quick question — do you have budget for this year allocated?' James: 'We do not have a specific line item yet.' SDR1: 'Okay, and who would be the decision-maker on something like this?' James (visibly irritated): 'It would depend on the scope.' The call ends in 12 minutes. Priya Ramaswamy opens: 'Before we get into what we do, I wanted to understand where Apex Logistics is right now with inventory analytics — is it an active pain point, or more on the radar for future?' James: 'Active — we expanded to two new depots this year and our reporting has not kept up.' Priya: 'That is exactly the scenario I was curious about. When you say it has not kept up, what does that look like on a Monday morning for your team?' 37 minutes later, Priya has full BANT context and a follow-up call scheduled with James and the CFO.

The difference is not the questions — it is the sequencing. SDR1 led with budget and authority before establishing need. James felt like a qualification target, not a business partner. Priya led with need, let the conversation surface authority and timeline naturally, and brought budget up in the context of impact.

The Diagnostic Conversation Sequence

Step 1: Establish need first — "What is the current state?" and "What is not working?" before anything else. The prospect describes their problem in their own words.

Step 2: Develop urgency — "What is driving the priority right now?" This reveals timeline naturally: if there is a business event, they will tell you. If they cannot answer, timeline is soft.

Step 3: Surface the decision-making unit — "Who else would be involved in making a decision like this?" rather than "Are you the decision-maker?" The former is collaborative; the latter puts them on the defensive.

Step 4: Budget as impact — "Have you had a chance to quantify what this is costing the business?" moves toward budget without asking if they have it. If they have quantified the cost, budget creation becomes a natural next step.

MEDDPICC — Enterprise Qualification at Scale

MEDDPICC is what BANT grows into when deal sizes go above £100,000 and sales cycles stretch to six months or longer. It is the framework used by the world's most sophisticated enterprise sales organisations.

MEDDPICC (sometimes MEDDIC) stands for: Metrics, Economic Buyer, Decision Criteria, Decision Process, Identify Pain, Champion, and Competition. Each element is a piece of intelligence about the deal — without which you are navigating in the dark.

M — Metrics

What is the quantified impact of solving this problem? Enterprise buyers do not buy because something feels right — they buy because the business case has been built. Metrics turn "this would be useful" into "this will generate £400,000 in recovered analyst time and reduce reporting errors by 80%." If the rep cannot state the Metrics, the champion cannot build the internal business case.

E — Economic Buyer

The Economic Buyer is the single person with final authority to release budget and approve the purchase. In mid-market, this is often the CFO or COO. In enterprise, it may be a VP or C-Suite exec you have never spoken to. The AE who has only spoken to the champion has not qualified the Economic Buyer — and can get surprised at procurement when the budget is pulled.

D — Decision Criteria

The formal and informal criteria by which the buying organisation will evaluate options. Formal: the RFP scoring matrix. Informal: the champion's personal priorities, the IT team's security requirements, the CFO's vendor risk concerns. Understanding all the criteria — not just the ones in the official process — is what allows you to shape them in your favour.

D — Decision Process

The exact sequence of steps, people, and gates between now and a signed contract. Who presents to whom, what approvals are needed, how long procurement takes, whether there is a legal review. Reps who do not map this process accurately miscalculate their close dates.

I — Identify Pain

Enterprise pain has three levels: the surface problem, the business impact, and the personal impact on the economic buyer. Surface: "our reporting is manual." Business impact: "we are making purchasing decisions on 48-hour-old data." Personal: "the CFO presented incorrect numbers to the board in Q2 and is under pressure." The deeper the pain, the more compelling the case for change.

C — Champion

The Champion is someone inside the buying organisation who wants you to win and will actively sell on your behalf. A champion is not someone who likes your product — they have power, credibility inside their organisation, and personal motivation to see this deal happen. Without a champion, you have a contact, not an ally.

C — Competition

Understanding the competitive landscape: who else is being evaluated, what their strengths and weaknesses are, and what the prospect has heard about each option. The rep who does not know the competition cannot position against it or counter comparative objections.

🎯 Expert Insight

The most underqualified MEDDPICC dimension is Champion. Most reps call someone a champion because they are helpful, responsive, and enthusiastic. A true champion has three characteristics: they have organisational credibility (people inside listen to them), they have personal motivation (this deal matters to their career or reputation), and they take action on your behalf (they schedule meetings, share internal documents, coach you on the political landscape). If your champion has not done all three, they are a sponsor, not a champion.

What Objections Actually Are

Most sales training treats objections as obstacles to overcome. That framing is wrong — and it creates adversarial dynamics that lose deals. Objections are diagnostic signals. They tell you exactly what is not yet true in the buyer's mind.

An objection is the buyer's way of saying: "I am not yet convinced that [X]." The words are a surface; the underlying concern is the signal. The rep who hears "it is too expensive" as a rejection is missing the real message: "I do not yet believe the value justifies the cost." That is solvable — if you know what it is.

The Four Types of Objection

1. Timing Objections — "We are not looking at this until next year." Timing objections are often hiding a lack of urgency. The prospect has no compelling reason to act now. The solution is not to pressure — it is to uncover the business event that makes now the right time.

2. Value Objections — "I am not sure this would be worth it for us." The prospect has not connected the problem to a cost they can quantify. This is a discovery failure — return to implication questions, not to the pitch.

3. Authority Objections — "I would need to run this past my CFO." You are in the wrong conversation — or you have not enabled your champion to navigate upwards. This signals incomplete BANT/MEDDPICC qualification.

4. Competitor Objections — "We are also looking at [competitor]." Healthy — and expected. The question is whether you understand the comparison criteria and whether your champion can position you favourably against the alternative.

The Objection Response Framework

Acknowledge: Never counter immediately. "I hear that — timing is important." (Acknowledging is not agreeing.)

Probe: Understand the specific concern behind the surface. "Can I ask what is driving the timing concern — is it a resource question, or is there a specific event making next year feel more realistic?"

Address: Respond to the specific concern — not the surface objection. Different probing answers require different responses.

Confirm: "Does that address the concern, or is there something I have missed?" Confirmation prevents circular objection loops.

🔴 Common Mistake

The most common objection-handling error is responding to the surface instead of the signal. A rep who hears 'too expensive' and immediately offers a discount has not addressed the concern — they have signalled that the original price was wrong. The right move is to probe: 'What would need to be true for the investment to make sense?' That question reopens the value conversation instead of starting a price negotiation.

Objection Prevention

The best objection handler in the building gets objections. The best sales process has already addressed every concern before it becomes an objection.

🏢 Case Study — Rebecca Walsh — The Prevention Approach

Rebecca Walsh has a policy: every common objection she has received in the past 12 months gets surfaced by her — before the prospect raises it. 'The instinct is to avoid the uncomfortable topic. But if I know they will eventually raise it, the worst thing I can do is let it arrive at a bad moment — like right before they are about to sign.' In a recent NorthPeak deal with a 200-person retailer: she knew IT would raise security concerns (they always do). In month two, she proactively introduced a 30-minute security review with the CISO — before IT was even involved in the evaluation. When IT finally joined: 'Oh, we already reviewed the security documentation with your team. We are satisfied.' Objection defused before it ever formed.

The Three-Stage Prevention Process

Stage 1: Build Your Objection Inventory — List every objection you have heard in the past 12 months. Categorise by type (timing, value, authority, competitor). Score by frequency and deal impact (high-frequency, high-impact = highest priority to prevent).

Stage 2: Map to Conversation Stage — Each objection arises at a predictable moment in the sales cycle. "We are not looking at this until next year" typically surfaces in discovery. "This is too expensive" typically surfaces at proposal. "I need to check with my CFO" surfaces at late stage. Map the objection to the stage where it first appears — that is where you need to address it proactively.

Stage 3: Pre-empt in the Conversation — Raise the concern yourself, before the prospect does: "One thing I want to make sure we address early — teams often raise questions about implementation resource and timeline. I want to go through that in detail today so it does not become a concern later in the process." This does three things: it signals confidence (you are not afraid of the concern), it demonstrates customer knowledge (you know what their team will raise), and it defuses the objection at a moment when both parties can address it calmly.

🎯 Expert Insight

The pricing objection is the most common and least prevented. Most reps wait until the prospect says 'this is too expensive' at the proposal stage — then they panic and discount. The prevention approach: build the ROI case with the prospect in discovery, so the number on the proposal is already justified before they see it. When the proposal arrives, the response is 'yes, we calculated that the recovery in analyst time alone covers the first year cost.' That is prevention. That is never discounting.

Rebecca proactively raises the security review with the CISO before IT is formally involved. What is the primary benefit?
Prevention timing is everything. The security objection raised at proposal stage (when the deal is weeks from closing) is far more damaging than the same concern addressed in month two during a calm technical review.
An AE consistently gets a pricing objection at proposal stage. According to the prevention framework, when should they address this?
The pricing objection at proposal stage is almost always a value objection in disguise — the prospect has not internalised the ROI case. Prevention means building that case with the prospect in discovery, so the proposal number is already justified before it is presented.
