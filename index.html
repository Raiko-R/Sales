Track 1 · Module 1 · Theory

The Consultative Mindset

Most reps lose deals before they even start — by pitching when they should be listening. This module is about the mindset shift that separates average AEs from the top performers.

The problem with pitching

Picture this: you get a 30-minute call with a Finance Manager at a 90-person consultancy. You spend the first 15 minutes walking through Flowline's features — time tracking, invoicing automation, the Xero integration. You know your product. You're confident.

The buyer says "interesting, we'll have a think" and goes quiet. You follow up three times. Nothing.

What went wrong? You answered questions they never asked. You solved problems you assumed they had. You talked about Flowline — not about them.

The core shift

A transactional seller asks: "How do I convince them to buy?"
A consultative seller asks: "What problem do they need solved, and am I the right person to solve it?"

What buyers actually want

Research by Gartner found that B2B buyers spend only 17% of their total purchase journey actually talking to sales reps. They've done research. They've formed opinions. By the time they're on a call with you, they don't want a product pitch — they want someone who understands their specific situation well enough to help them make a confident decision.

The consultative seller becomes a trusted advisor. They ask better questions. They listen more than they talk. They make the buyer feel understood — not sold to.

❌ Transactional
"Flowline integrates with Xero and automatically generates invoices, which saves your team hours every week."
✓ Consultative
"Walk me through how your team currently handles invoicing — where does it tend to break down for you?"

The 70/30 rule

In a consultative discovery call, the buyer should be talking 70% of the time. You talk 30% — and most of that is asking questions. If you find yourself presenting in the first 15 minutes, you've moved too fast.

Your job in discovery isn't to be impressive. It's to be curious.

Key principle: The best discovery calls end with the buyer saying "this is exactly what I needed to talk through" — before you've mentioned a single feature. That's the consultative mindset working.

Track 1 · Module 2 · Theory

Discovery & SPIN Selling

You've mastered the consultative mindset. Now you need the question framework that turns curiosity into structured discovery. SPIN is how top AEs uncover pain without interrogating buyers.

Why most discovery calls fail

AEs ask too many closed questions too fast. "Do you have a time tracking problem?" "Are you happy with your invoicing?" These shut buyers down. They get a yes or no, and learn nothing. SPIN replaces guessing with a sequence that progressively deepens the conversation.

The SPIN Framework

Four question types, in sequence. Each layer builds on the last.

S
Situation
Establish facts about the buyer's current state. Use sparingly — too many and it feels like an interrogation.
P
Problem
Surface difficulties and frustrations. "Where does this tend to break down?" — open, non-leading.
I
Implication
Explore the consequences of the problem. This is where urgency is created — without pressure.
N
Need-Payoff
Let the buyer articulate the value of solving it. They sell themselves — you just guide.

SPIN in a Flowline discovery call

Here's how all four types play out in a single conversation with Sarah Patel, Finance Manager:

S — Situation
"How many tools does your team currently use to get from a completed project to a sent invoice?"
P — Problem
"Where does that process tend to go wrong — is it data collection, the reconciliation, or something else?"
I — Implication
"When an invoice goes out with an error, what does that typically mean for the client relationship — and your team's time to fix it?"
N — Need-Payoff
"If you could close month-end in one day instead of five, what would that free your team up to actually focus on?"
The rule of implication questions: Don't rush to Need-Payoff. Most AEs skip straight there and get shallow answers. Spend time on Implication — when the buyer feels the real cost of their problem, they are already half-sold before you say anything about your product.

Prepare your SPIN bank before every call

Before each discovery call, write three questions for each SPIN category tailored to what you know about the prospect. You won't use them all — but having them ready means you never freeze up. The best AEs sound spontaneous because they've prepared thoroughly.

Active Listening & Note-Taking

Asking good SPIN questions is only half the job. Most AEs listen just well enough to ask their next question. Top performers listen to understand — and they know exactly what to capture and why.

The three levels of listening

Not all listening is equal. Research in sales coaching identifies three distinct layers, and most reps operate at level one.

1
Surface (words)
You hear what the buyer says and retain the literal content. Most reps stay here. They remember features mentioned, not feelings.
2
Interpretive (meaning)
You understand what they're really saying beyond the words. "We manage" often means "we're in pain but don't want to admit it."
3
Empathic (emotion)
You sense how they feel about the situation. Sarah says "we had a £12k error last quarter" flatly — but she's embarrassed. That matters for how you position the solution.
0
Preoccupied
You're half-listening while planning your next question or pitch. Common. Fatal. Buyers notice immediately.

What to capture during a call

Your notes are the foundation of your next step — a follow-up email, a proposal, a demo. If you can't reference what the buyer said in your own words, you sound like you weren't listening.

Capture these 5 things
PAINThe specific problem they described, in their language — not your summary of it.
STAKESWhat it costs them: time, money, reputation, relationships.
WHOEvery stakeholder mentioned — names, titles, opinions.
TIMELINEAny date signals: budget cycles, upcoming reviews, expiring contracts.
SIGNALSBuying signals ("can you send the contract?") and competitor mentions.

The post-call summary email

Send within 30 minutes of hanging up. The summary email does two things: it proves you listened, and it locks in the next step. Structure it in three paragraphs: what you heard, what you're sending, and what happens next.

Summary email structure

Para 1 — What I heard: Reflect their problem back in their own language. No features, no pitch.
Para 2 — What I'm sending: Relevant case study, ROI estimate, or proposal — whatever was agreed.
Para 3 — Next step: Date, time, attendees, agenda. Already in the calendar.

What to skip: Don't transcribe everything. Ignore small talk, filler phrases, and hypotheticals ("if we were ever going to do something like this..."). If they didn't commit to it, don't record it as a signal.

Building Rapport Without Being Pushy

Most rapport advice sounds like a charm school syllabus. Remember their kids' names. Mirror their body language. Fake enthusiasm. None of it works on experienced B2B buyers. Real rapport is built on one thing: demonstrating that you're competent and worth their time.

Rapport is a function of competence, not personality

Think about the best sales conversations you've had — on either side of the table. The rep who earned your trust fastest wasn't the most likeable. They were the most prepared. They asked questions that proved they understood your world. They didn't waste your time.

This is what "rapport through competence" means: the buyer relaxes because they can see you know what you're talking about and you're not going to pitch them something irrelevant.

The 60-second trust signal

The first 60 seconds of a call sets the frame. Most reps open with "So, let me tell you a bit about us..." — which immediately positions them as a salesperson with an agenda. Instead, use a three-part frame-setter:

The 3-part opener
1
Set the agenda: "I'd like to spend about 20 minutes asking you some questions about how you currently manage X. Is that fair?"
2
Remove the sales threat: "I'm not going to pitch anything today. If what I hear doesn't fit, I'll tell you that."
3
Signal respect for their time: "I've done my homework on your firm — I have a few specific questions I'd like your take on."

Mirror and match — the real version

Mirroring in B2B doesn't mean copying someone's body language. It means calibrating your energy, pace, and language register to match the buyer's.

If Marcus Chen is terse and gives short answers, don't compensate with enthusiasm — match his directness. If Sarah Patel is reflective and detailed, slow down and follow her lead. Buyers who feel "got" open up.

✗ Fake rapport
"I love that — what a great point, Sarah! Totally agree. So our platform basically..."
✓ Real rapport
"That's useful — the internal credibility piece is often more acute than the financial one. How long has that been the situation?"
What rapport is not: scripted openers, complimenting their LinkedIn post, asking about their weekend, or over-thanking them for their time. Buyers see through all of it. The fastest way to build trust is to ask one question that proves you did your homework.
